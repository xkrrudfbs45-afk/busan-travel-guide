Gupo Guksu: How a Busan Noodle Town Was Made

A humble bowl of noodles tells a story of transport, grain, migration and the market town of Gupo.

GO2BUSAN LOCAL GUIDELOCAL FOOD HISTORYHISTORY & PRACTICAL TIPS

LOCAL FOOD HISTORY · This guide separates documented history from local stories whose details remain uncertain.

A noodle named after a place

Gupo guksu refers both to dried noodles produced around Gupo in northern Busan and to dishes made with them. The familiar bowl is often served with anchovy broth and toppings such as seaweed, chives and finely cut pickled radish. The name matters: “Gupo” became a place-based food identity rather than simply the name of one restaurant.

From grain port to noodle workshops

The Academy of Korean Studies traces Gupo’s importance to its position on the Nakdong River, where grain and goods moved through a busy trading area. Rice mills, flour mills and noodle workshops expanded around the railway and market district during the colonial period. By the mid-20th century, the town had become closely associated with dried noodles.

War, flour aid and the noodle boom

During the Korean War, refugees gathered in Busan and flour arrived through relief supplies. Noodles became an accessible way to feed many people. In the 1960s and 1970s, dozens of small noodle factories operated in Gupo; strands were spread out to dry in yards and along market lanes. Local accounts connect the noodles’ springy texture and slight saltiness to the region’s sun and breezes from the river and coast.

A name that became a shared heritage

Gupo guksu later became the subject of a trademark dispute over whether one maker could monopolize the geographic name. The legal and industry history helped reinforce the idea that the name belongs to a wider local tradition, not just one producer. Today, the Gupo Market area is the natural place to explore the story, although the number of traditional factories has fallen sharply.
