Busan Dwaeji-gukbap: A Bowl Shaped by Refugee History

A hearty bowl became a local staple amid war, migration and the need for affordable meals—but its origin story is not as simple as one date.

What is Busan dwaeji-gukbap?

Dwaeji-gukbap combines pork and rice with a broth commonly made from pork bones. In Busan, it is often served with chives, seasoning paste and salted shrimp so diners can adjust the flavour. Broth colour and richness vary by shop.

Why the Korean War is part of the story

A Yonhap report drawing on city sources and local historians describes how pork bones available around U.S. military facilities were used to make nourishing soup, and how pork-soup restaurants spread through Busan during the 1950s and 1960s. The Seomyeon area, near the former Hialeah Camp, became associated with a cluster of restaurants. This is a widely reported account of the dish’s postwar growth, not proof that every version of pork soup began at the same time or place.

One dish, several regional histories

Pork soup is not unique to Busan; related dishes are eaten elsewhere in southeastern Korea. The Busan version is associated with local restaurant culture, long-simmered pork-bone broth in many shops, and the familiar combination of chives, seasoning and rice. Older regional soups, migration and postwar scarcity all form part of the discussion, so one simplified origin story should not be treated as settled fact.

How to order

Dwaeji-gukbap is the standard pork soup with rice. Naeyang-gukbap includes offal; seokkeo-gukbap mixes cuts or ingredients; sundae-gukbap includes Korean blood sausage. Add chives, seasoning paste and salted shrimp gradually, tasting as you go.
