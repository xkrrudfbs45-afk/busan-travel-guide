FOOD HISTORY · PORT CITY

Busan Eomuk: The Port-City History of Korean Fish Cake

A familiar skewer in a paper cup has a deeper story: fish, markets, migration, small factories and the changing food culture of a busy port.

Local perspective · More than a street snack. “Busan eomuk” describes a regional food and production tradition, not one single recipe or company. This guide focuses on the documented wider history.

What makes Busan eomuk distinctive?

Eomuk is made by seasoning and shaping fish paste, then cooking it by frying, steaming or other methods. In Busan, it appears as market snacks, skewers in broth, side dishes and modern bakery-style fish cakes. Texture, fish content and seasoning vary by maker, so “Busan eomuk” is not one fixed product.

The colonial-era connection

The Academy of Korean Studies’ Encyclopedia of Korean Culture traces the modern Busan fish-cake industry partly to kamaboko, a Japanese fish-paste food. During Japanese colonial rule, Japanese-run fish-cake shops operated around Bupyeong Market in what is now Jung-gu. This is an important part of the industry’s history, but it should not be mistaken for the whole story of Korean fish-cake cooking, which has older regional food precedents.

After liberation: local production takes shape

The encyclopedia records the establishment of Donggwang Foods after liberation as an early Korean-owned fish-cake factory. Busan’s access to fish and its dense urban market made it a practical place for production and distribution. Small workshops and market-based sellers helped turn fish paste into an affordable everyday food.

The Korean War and a growing market

During the Korean War, large numbers of displaced people arrived in Busan. The encyclopedia links this population growth and the availability of fish to the expansion of local eomuk production. In the 1950s, makers around Yeongdo’s Bongnae Market and Yeongju-dong became part of the industry’s story; production later spread to other market districts, including Choryang. This history connects eomuk with the realities of feeding a crowded port city, not just with today’s tourist snack stalls.

From market food to a modern food brand

For decades, eomuk was sold as an inexpensive snack or side dish. More recently, some makers have developed premium products, gift boxes, fish-cake croquettes and shop formats that resemble bakeries. The change in presentation reflects a wider shift in how Busan’s food heritage is marketed, while the core idea—making use of local fish and processing it into an accessible food—remains recognisable.

How to explore the story in Busan

Markets and long-running producers are useful places to notice how eomuk is made, sold and eaten. Yeongdo’s Bongnae Market, the Choryang area and the historic market district around Bupyeong each connect to different chapters of Busan’s food and commerce. Check individual shop details before visiting; the history of the industry should not be confused with a guarantee that a specific business is still operating or unchanged.
