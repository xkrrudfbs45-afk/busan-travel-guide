Busan Dwaeji-gukbap: A Bowl Shaped by Refugee History

A hearty bowl became a local staple amid war, migration and the need for affordable meals—but its origin story is not as simple as one date.

What is Busan dwaeji-gukbap?

Dwaeji-gukbap combines pork and rice with a broth commonly made from pork bones. In Busan, it is often served with chives, seasoning paste and salted shrimp so diners can adjust the flavour. Broth colour and richness vary by shop.

Why the Korean War is part of the story

A Yonhap report drawing on city sources and local historians describes how pork bones available around U.S. military facilities were used to make nourishing soup, and how pork-soup restaurants spread through Busan during the 1950s and 1960s. The Seomyeon area, near the former Hialeah Camp, became associated with a cluster of restaurants. This is a widely reported account of the dish’s postwar growth, not proof that every version of pork soup began at the same time or place.

One dish, several regional histories

Pork soup is not unique to Busan; related dishes are eaten elsewhere in southeastern Korea. The Busan version is associated with local restaurant culture, long-simmered pork-bone broth in many shops, and the familiar combination of chives, seasoning and rice. Older regional soups, migration and postwar scarcity all form part of the discussion, so one simplified origin story should not be treated as settled fact.

Order with confidence: suyuk baekban vs. ttaro gukbap

These menu names describe different ways the meal is served. Ttaro gukbap (따로국밥) means the rice is served separately from the soup; the pork is still generally served in the broth. Suyuk baekban (수육백반) is a set meal with a bowl of soup and rice plus a separate plate of suyuk—slices of boiled pork. Visit Busan and Korea Tourism Organization restaurant listings show both dishes as distinct menu options.

At some restaurants, suyuk baekban comes with lettuce or other wrap vegetables and dipping sauces; side dishes differ by shop, so do not assume every set includes the same extras. Try the pork first, then add a little salted shrimp or seasoning to the broth and adjust to taste.

Useful Korean phrases:

  • 수육백반 하나 주세요. — One suyuk baekban, please.
  • 따로국밥 하나 주세요. — One ttaro gukbap, please.
  • 밥은 따로 나오나요? — Is the rice served separately?
  • 고기는 어떤 부위가 들어가나요? — Which cut of pork is included?

Menu names and set contents can vary by restaurant. Check the menu board and ask before ordering, especially if you avoid offal or have dietary restrictions.

Decode the meat options before you order

Seok-eo gukbap (섞어국밥) usually means a mixed bowl, but the exact combination varies and may include pork slices and offal. Naeyang gukbap (내장국밥) is the offal-focused option. “Salkogi” is not a guaranteed standard menu option at every restaurant.

For lean pork only, show: 살코기만 넣어 주세요. 내장은 빼 주세요. — “Lean pork only, please. No offal.” Ask 어떤 부위가 들어가나요? (“Which cuts are included?”) before ordering. Menu names and recipes differ by shop.

Other common gukbap menu names

Dwaeji-gukbap is the standard pork soup with rice. Naeyang-gukbap includes offal; seokkeo-gukbap mixes cuts or ingredients; sundae-gukbap includes Korean blood sausage. Add chives, seasoning paste and salted shrimp gradually, tasting as you go.

Read the menu: mixed pork or lean meat?

Seok-eo-gukbap (섞어국밥) usually means a mixed bowl with pork slices and may include offal or other cuts. Recipes and menu labels vary by restaurant, so ask what is included if you do not eat offal.

Salkogi-gukbap (살코기국밥) is the clearer choice when you prefer lean pork meat rather than offal. You can ask: 살코기만 들어가나요? — “Does this contain only lean meat?” If you want the rice served separately, order ttaro-gukbap (따로국밥); this describes how the rice is served, not the type of meat.

Useful phrase: 내장 빼고 살코기만 넣어 주세요. — “Please leave out the offal and use pork meat only.” Confirm directly with the restaurant because names and recipes differ.
